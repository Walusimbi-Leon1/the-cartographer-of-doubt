### Story 2 — The Cartographer of Conversations

There is a desk in the attic of the house on Thornapple Lane, and on that desk there are maps. Not maps of countries or coastlines—those are too certain, too agreed upon. These are maps of conversations. Of arguments that ended badly. Of silences that stretched too long. Of the words she should have said and the ones she said instead.

Mara draws them in pencil first, then inks them in with a fine-tipped pen she buys by the dozen from the stationer's on Cobb Street. The stationer, a man with a glass eye and a talent for knowing what people need before they do, had asked her once what she was mapping.

"Interiors," she'd said.

He'd nodded as if this explained everything, which it did, in a way. She'd been buying pens from him for eleven years and he'd never once asked about her work beyond that first question. She appreciated this about him. She'd thought, more than once, that if she could draw a map of the conversation she'd had with him, it would be a simple, elegant thing—two lines that ran parallel and never tangled.

The maps themselves are complicated. They have to be.

---

The first map she ever drew was of a conversation with her mother, six years ago. Her mother had called to tell her that the cancer had moved to her bones, and Mara had said, "I'll book a flight," and her mother had said, "There's no need," and Mara had said, "I want to," and her mother had said, "I know," and that had been the end of it.

The conversation had lasted four minutes and seventeen seconds. Mara knew this because she'd checked her phone afterward. She'd sat on the floor of her kitchen—the floor was cold, she remembered that—and she'd tried to replay the conversation in her head, to find the moment where she could have said something different. Something truer.

She'd found three such moments.

The first was right after "I'll book a flight." She could have said, "I'm afraid." The second was after "There's no need." She could have said, "I need it. I need to see you." The third was after "I know." She could have said, "I love you," but her mother knew that, and saying it would have been for Mara's benefit, not her mother's, and so she'd stayed silent.

She'd drawn that conversation as a series of corridors. Her mother's voice was a wide hallway with doors on either side, and Mara's voice was a narrow passage that dead-ended three times. She'd spent an hour on it, shading the dead-ends with a graphite stick, and when she'd finished, she'd felt something she hadn't expected.

She'd felt seen.

---

The maps have rules, though she didn't write them down anywhere. She keeps them in her head, the way a cartographer keeps the principles of projection—the knowledge that any flat map of a round world is a lie, and that the only question is which lies you choose to tell.

Rule one: the map must be beautiful. This is non-negotiable. She draws the coastlines of her cowardice, the mountain ranges of her anger, the rivers of her longing. She colors them with watercolor washes, pale blues and ochres and a green she mixed once and has never been able to replicate. She does not draw the maps to punish herself. She draws them to understand the terrain.

Rule two: the map must be honest. This is harder. She has to include the things she's ashamed of—the sharp retorts, the hasty exits, the lies she told to keep the peace. She draws them as fault lines, as cliffs, as places where the ground gives way.

Rule three: the map must show the conversation as it could have been. This is the rule that keeps her up at night. She draws the alternate routes in red ink, fine and tentative, like the veins in a leaf. Sometimes the alternate routes are beautiful—wide avenues leading to places of warmth and understanding. Sometimes they're dead-ends too, and she marks them with a small X, the way an explorer marks an impassable peak.

---

The map of the conversation with her mother sits in a drawer to the left of her desk. She hasn't looked at it in years. She doesn't need to. She knows every contour of it by heart.

Her mother died nine days after that phone call. Mara had not taken a flight. She'd taken three—one to the city, one to the town, one to the village where her mother had chosen to live out her final years in a house with a garden that had gone to seed. She'd arrived a day and a half before the end. She'd sat by the bed and held her mother's hand, and she'd said, "I'm here," and her mother had said, "I know," and this time the words had been enough.

After the funeral, she'd gone through her mother's things. She'd found a box of photographs, each one labeled on the back in her mother's neat handwriting—*Mara, age four, first day of school*; *Mara, age ten, with the dog*; *Mara, age sixteen, before the dance*. On the bottom of the box, beneath the photographs, was a folded piece of paper.

It was a map. Her mother had drawn it. It showed a house, and a garden, and a path leading to a gate, and beyond the gate, a series of roads that led to places she hadn't labeled. In the top right corner, in her mother's hand, were the words: *The way home.*

Mara had kept the map. It was in the drawer now, beneath the map of their last conversation.

---

She draws a map that evening, after the rain stops. The conversation she's mapping is one she had with her brother, Daniel, at their mother's funeral. He'd said, "You were always her favorite," and she'd said, "That's not true," and he'd said, "You got to leave. You got to go to the city and become someone. I stayed. I stayed and I took care of her, and you got to just—leave."

She'd said, "I'm sorry," and he'd said, "Sorry doesn't bring back the years," and they'd stood in silence in the church hall, surrounded by people who thought they were grieving together.

She draws this conversation as an archipelago. Daniel's voice is a chain of islands, each one a separate grievance, and her voice is a boat that never quite reaches the shore. She draws the red ink routes—the things she could have said. She could have said, "I stayed because I couldn't bear to watch her die slowly. You stayed because you couldn't bear to leave her. We both made choices, Daniel. We both paid." She could have said, "She loved you too. She told me. She told me you were the one who made her proudest." She could have said, "I'm not the person you think I am."

She draws all of these routes, and they all lead to the same place: a small bay on the largest island, where she imagines Daniel's face softening, where she imagines him saying, "I know. I know you did what you could." She colors the bay a deep blue, the color of water at dusk.

She doesn't send him the map. She never sends them. They're not for the people they depict. They're for her, for the part of her that needs to see the terrain in order to navigate it. She's thought about this a lot, about why she draws them, and she's come to a conclusion: she draws them because the conversations she wishes she'd had are as real to her as the ones she actually did. They're just as much a part of her. They shaped her just as deeply. And they deserve to be recorded.

---

The stranger appears in town that week. He's a mapmaker, or so he tells people—a man who draws maps of places that have been forgotten, that have never been officially recorded. He sets up a table in the market square, and he draws maps for anyone who asks, free of charge.

Mara watches him from the bakery window. She's there to buy bread, but she lingers, watching him bent over his paper, his pen moving in sure strokes. He's older than her, with gray in his beard and deep lines around his eyes. He looks like a man who has seen a lot of terrain.

She doesn't approach him. Instead, she goes home and draws a map of the conversation she might have with him. She draws it before she's had it, which is new. She draws a bridge—a single, elegant span across a river she labels *fear*. On the far side, she draws a clearing, and in the clearing, two chairs, and on the chairs, two figures. Her and the mapmaker. She draws the conversation as a series of paths leading to the clearing, and she colors them all in red.

She looks at the map for a long time. Then she folds it and puts it in her pocket.

The next day, she goes to the market square. The mapmaker is there again. She stands in front of his table, and she says, "I draw maps too."

He looks up. His eyes are the color of the sea in winter. "What do you map?"

"Conversations," she says. "The ones I wish I'd had."

He doesn't look surprised. He looks like a man who has heard stranger things. "That's a difficult terrain," he says.

"It is," she says. "But I'm learning to navigate it."

He gestures to the chair across from his table. "Sit," he says. "Tell me about the landscape."

She sits. She doesn't take the map out of her pocket. She doesn't need to. She already knows the way.

---

That night, she draws the map of the conversation she actually had with the mapmaker. It's different from the one she'd imagined. He'd told her about the places he'd mapped—a village that had been drowned for a reservoir, a road that had been consumed by the forest, a mountain that had been moved, stone by stone, for a highway. He'd told her that the best maps were of things that were almost gone, because they required the most attention.

She'd told him about her mother. About Daniel. About the desk in the attic and the drawer full of conversations she'd never had.

He'd listened. That was the remarkable thing. He'd listened the way a cartographer listens to the land—with patience, with attention, with the understanding that the truth was in the details.

She draws this conversation as a river. Not a simple river, but a braided one, splitting and rejoining, splitting and rejoining. She colors it blue and green and, in the places where the braids are deepest, a dark, translucent indigo.

At the bottom of the map, she writes a single word in the margin: *Possible*.

She doesn't know what it means yet. She doesn't need to. The map will tell her, in time. That's what maps are for.
